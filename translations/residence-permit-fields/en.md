Why put extra fields on documents beyond the bare essentials?

The more fields, the more room for errors and the more queues.
Over any trivial little detail you have to reapply and get everything issued all over again.

We got Serbian residence permits. Besides the photo, full name, date of birth, expiry date and card number, the cards also list citizenship, place of birth and other nonsense. The chip encodes your home address and a whole lot of other junk.

Why is all of this on the plastic?

Why can't this data be kept on a server, in a database, where it can be changed? And the record is easy to pull up by the card number. The number is unique; it's enough to establish that I am me and to tell me apart from a namesake, etc.

You're going to check against the database anyway. How is someone without internet access going to verify that my place of birth says Uzbekistan?

Naturally, when my wife's residence permit was issued, the wrong registered address was written to the chip. And my card got the wrong place of birth. My wife got off with having the chip re-encoded on the spot. I had to have my card reissued.

The Serbian residence permit card also states the "grounds" on which it was granted. And you won't believe it! Those change over the course of your life. And guess what? In that case you have to get the permit all over again! Which means getting in line, submitting your data again, filling out an application, copying a pile of documents and bringing them to the police. There, inspectors shuffle these documents around and pass them on to others, who type the data from the printed documents into an electronic database, from which they print me a waiting ticket. After that they deliberate, and after some indefinite time I come to pick up exactly the same card, only now one field is pretty and correct!

I'm not saying the state shouldn't know where I live. I get a separate registration document anyway. And this data is in my profile. I'd even report a move online, but that wouldn't be nicely printed on plastic, which means it's all worthless — it carries no weight whatsoever!

Meanwhile the inspectors can't keep up with all this "useful" work, and the queues pile up: queues to apply, queues to collect...

On top of that, documents are issued with a strictly limited validity period instead of "until the legal grounds for residing in the country cease to exist", and once again queues build up to reissue cards with the same supporting documents, on the same grounds.

When I applied for permanent residence in Serbia (it's the same thing as a residence permit, just called something else, and the card is pink instead of yellow), I was turned down, even though I held a valid residence permit that let me live in the country. But my "grounds" had temporarily changed, and you couldn't just update the grounds in a database row and carry on with the procedure. They were supposed to take my residence permit card away, but they couldn't, because printed on it is...

I had to stand in long queues: for the refusal, to apply, for the review, for fingerprinting and to collect the card. The queues are long because the inspectors can't cope with the workload.

And just the other day we got Spanish residence permits, and my wife's has the wrong registered address — printed right on the card. Why? (Besides, before that we had already received separate documents about our place of residence.) You won't believe it, but people sometimes move! And people who don't own property in the country are quite likely to change their address while their residence permit is still valid. And what will you have to do after moving? Get in line? And how far out are the appointment slots for submitting documents right now? Six months ahead?

Because everyone's busy with real work.

Another example. In many countries, license plates show the region where the car was registered. A vestige of the past. Firstly, in an analog world where you can't run a car through a database, you can tell at a glance where this out-of-towner has come from to flood the capital, and stoke furious anger between the locals and the country bumpkins. A very useful division.

And when you move from one city to another — that happens too — you have to change your car's plates along with your registered address and the data on your residence permit.

In Spain, license plates aren't tied to regions: they're four random digits and three random letters. If you buy a car in Madrid and register it in Bilbao, all you need is an in-person vehicle inspection. Europe hasn't yet learned to do a full vehicle inspection from three photos of the car, the way Russia does. There's room to grow.

The end!
