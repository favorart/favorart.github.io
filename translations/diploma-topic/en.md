More than 10 years have passed since I defended my bachelor's thesis. Back then I dreamed of writing something close to self-modifying code. Of course, it turned out far more modest: an interpreted language, #IPL, whose syntax "resembles" formalized natural-language text, and whose programs it could "translate" into different languages. In practice (back then), source code could only be understood by a specialist, and I wanted to bring programming closer to people.

Too bad I didn't know in my undergrad years that "this was already promised back in 1959". Ironic!

"COBOL (1959) was promoted as a language in which business managers would write programs themselves: ADD TAX TO PRICE GIVING TOTAL." In the end it was the same old programmers who wrote in it, just more verbosely.

Then "analysts will write their own SQL queries" (which, by the way, is a reality today with the help of AI).

"The future belongs to no-code platforms!"

It didn't work out back then, because natural language is ambiguous and a compiler demands precision.

Today I open a terminal, tell an AI agent in my ordinary language what needs to be done, and it does it, fixing errors along the way. That's both translation from a natural language into a formal one and code that changes in response to the result. The problem I had barely started on in my thesis is now being solved in everyday life! At last there's a "compiler" that can ask follow-up questions, understands context and handles ambiguity calmly.

My speculative assumption (from back then, but I believe in it even more now): natural language should become the main programming language. Formal programming languages will fade into the past, just as machine code and assembly did.

The agent corrects me: "they won't disappear, they'll move down the stack", arguing that people write drivers, bootloaders and hot loops in assembly; most just never see it. And I agree: formal languages (some of them) (for now) are still being looked at during debugging and review.  😅

**Master's thesis: scientific novelty with a week left on it**

My master's thesis, #RobotHand, was about teaching a robot to use its iron body correctly to reach the goals it's given, without knowing its own structure and capabilities, by trial and error, like a newborn in its first days.

This work still has a pinch of novelty left in it. But honestly, my gut feeling is that finishing it properly with Claude would take about a week.

It's amazing that in such a short time I've witnessed such a cosmic leap in how effective our tools are!

Do you have a student project that went from science to routine in a couple of years?
