> "A #ban is a person's true freedom."
>
> Ye. B. Mizulina, Senator of the Federation Council, 2019

It's hard to shake the feeling that we're living through the last happy years of the open global Internet. For now a #VPN still helps get around the barriers, but you can see the shift from blocklists to allowlists: from banned traffic to permitted traffic. The network's decentralized, fault-tolerant architecture can survive partial delinking on any scale. But, as everyone knows, there's nothing a clever person can invent that a fool can't break.

Today technology lets you work and run a business remotely, wherever you happen to be. The technical capability is there, but political will blocks it.

Besides the example that's all too familiar to a Russian-speaking audience and the #Chinese Great Firewall, there are others:

\#Turkey periodically restricts access to VPNs and messaging apps during periods of social tension. Banks cut back their apps' functionality for customers outside the country and demand an in-person visit to confirm transactions.

\#Uzbekistan is also going through a digital transformation: many things can already be done online. Uzkomnazorat, a communications watchdog, has been set up. Personal and biometric data are collected in a single repository (it's unclear whether a state-run one or one handed over to a private company). This data is used to calculate ratings and loyalty scores — you can learn a lot of new things about yourself. A large part of economic and private life has ended up in a database to which all sorts of services are connected via a single secure protocol: notaries, payment services, ministries and so on.

Uzbekistan is following the general trend. The #EU, after electronic visas, is moving toward a digital wallet (eIDAS 2.0) — a single window for accessing government and important private services: banks, transport, healthcare. But there are risks too: in Uzbekistan in 2026, rumors circulated about a leak of millions of citizens' data. They were officially denied, with only the fact of cyberattacks and a breach of about 60,000 records acknowledged. After that, access to Uzbek services from abroad was restricted.

Out of the blue, I wanted to check my credit history in Uzbekistan. I couldn't get into my account at the credit bureau. I managed to log in to the government services portal, but not to use any of its services. The alternative is the KATM mobile app, but I couldn't download it: it isn't available in my Google account's region.

Citizens of #Russia are already used to turning on a VPN from abroad to pay their utility bills or a traffic fine. This skill comes in handy for residents of other countries too.

Geoblocking isn't new: many #US government websites (on local benefits and taxes) and the sites of large companies block foreign traffic. It's cheaper than building a sophisticated cyberdefense system.

I'd like to end with a parable about a shovel and its handle:

A worker was told to dig a hole.

"Here's a handle," says the boss.

"Where's the blade?"

"Blades are handed out on Thursdays, but that's when the handles get collected for inventory."

"But I can't dig!"

"If we gave you everything at once, you'd dig the hole in an hour and sit around doing nothing. This way you're busy all day: either polishing the wood or sharpening the steel. The process is under way."
